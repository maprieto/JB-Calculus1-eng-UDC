2.5. Continuity#

2.5.1. Continuity at a point#

Definition

Let \(f:A\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\), \(x_0\in A\). We will say that \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\) if and only if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to x_0} f(x)\) exists and that limit is \(f(x_0)\).

That is, \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\) if and only if

\[ \forall\epsilon>0, \exists\delta>0 \Big/ \left|x-x_0\right|<\delta\Rightarrow\left|f(x)-f(x_0)\right|<\epsilon. \]

Let us observe that, with respect to the definition of limit, there is a small difference. Before we wrote

\[ 0< \left|x-x_0\right|<\delta, \]

whereas in the case of continuity we simply write

\[ \left|x-x_0\right|<\delta. \]

Why? When we talk about limit, we are interested in the point \(x_0\) not taking part in the definition, so that we can have limits of functions that are not even defined at \(x_0\). Hence the condition \(0<|x-x_0|\) (obviously, \(0=|x-x_0|\) if and only if \(x=x_0\)). However, in the case of continuity, including the point \(x_0\) is natural. Moreover, for continuity it cannot spoil anything, because if \(x=x_0\) then \(|f(x)-f(x_0)|\) is \(0\), so it will always be smaller than any positive \(\epsilon\).

In the following figure we show graphically the possible cases in which continuity fails. The first two (\(\not\exists f(x_0)\), \(\displaystyle\not\exists\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)\)) are called essential discontinuities, whereas the third case (\(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)\not=f(x_0)\)) is a removable discontinuity.

A touch of humor, taken from the Twitter account @MathMatize:

Some important properties of continuity are the following.

Property (Algebra of continuous functions)

Let \(f,g:A\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) be functions continuous at a point \(x_0\in A\). Then

  • \(\lambda f\) is continuous at \(x_0\), \(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\),

  • \(f\pm g\) is continuous at \(x_0\),

  • \(fg\) is continuous at \(x_0\),

  • if \(g(x_0)\not=0\), \(\frac{f}{g}\) is continuous at \(x_0\).

Property

The composition of continuous functions is a continuous function. That is,

\[\begin{split} \left.\begin{array}{l} f:A\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} \text{ continuous at } x_0\in A \\ g:f(A)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ continuous at }f(x_0) \end{array}\right\}\Longrightarrow g\circ f:A\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} \text{ continuous at }x_0\in A. \end{split}\]

Property

The limit commutes with continuous functions. That is, let \(f\) and \(g\) be functions such that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=l\in\mathbb{R}\) exists and \(g\) is a function continuous at \(l\). Then

\[ \lim_{x\to x_0}g\left(f(x)\right)=g\left(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)\right)=g(l). \]

Thus, for example,

\[ \lim_{x\to 1}\sin\left(\frac{\ln x+\cos^2 x}{e^x+2x}\right)=\sin\left(\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{\ln x+\cos^2 x}{e^x+2x}\right), \]

since the sine function is continuous.

2.5.2. Continuity on intervals#

Now that we have continuity at a point well defined, let us define continuity on intervals, both open and closed.

Definition

  1. Let \(f:(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\). We will say that \(f\) is continuous on \((a,b)\) if it is continuous at every point of \((a,b)\).

  2. Let \(f:[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\). We will say that \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) if

    a. \(f\) is continuous on \((a,b)\),

    b. \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) from the right,

    c. \(f\) is continuous at \(b\) from the left.

Definition

Let \(f:A\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\). We will say that \(x_0\in A\) is a root of \(f\) if \(f(x_0)=0\).

2.5.3. Bolzano’s and Weierstrass’s theorems#

Theorem (Bolzano’s theorem)

Let \(f:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function on \([a,b]\) such that \(f(a)f(b)<0\). Then there exists \(x_0\in(a,b)\) such that \(f(x_0)=0\).

Bolzano’s theorem speaks about the existence of roots for continuous functions. We should make some comments about it:

  1. There may be several roots, as shown in the graph on the right in the previous figure.

  2. If any of the hypotheses is removed, the theorem is generally no longer valid, as shown in the following figure.

Theorem (Weierstrass’s theorem)

Let \(f:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function on \([a,b]\). Then \(f\) attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on \([a,b]\). That is,

\[ \exists x_1, x_2\in[a,b]\Big/ f(x_1)\leq f(x)\leq f(x_2)\qquad\forall x\in[a,b]. \]
